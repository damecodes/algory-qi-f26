# Homework 8: Price an option from history alone

All numbers come from hw08_starter.py, run on Sunday 2026-11-15, so the last close in the data is Friday 2026-11-13. The full printed output is in hw08_output.txt and the plots are saved separately as q2_return_distribution.png, q4_final_prices.png and q6_strike_comparison.png. The price history used is saved in the data folder, so running the script with the --snapshot flag reproduces every number below.

The data is 20 years of daily SPY closes from yfinance, 2006-11-15 to 2026-11-13, which is 5,030 trading days. I used the raw close rather than the dividend-adjusted close. An option on SPY pays off on the traded price, and that price drops by the dividend on every ex-date, so returns that include dividends would credit a call holder with about 1.1% a year that they never receive. The last close in the data is $754.37, but every price below uses the spot of $760.88 given in the assignment. Applying returns to a spot only scales the answers, so the difference does not change any conclusion.

## Q1. The 21-day return distribution

The function n_day_returns takes the price series and a window length N and returns every overlapping N-day simple return, one window for each starting day that has N days after it. A series of L prices therefore gives L minus N windows, and with N equal to 1 it is exactly the daily return series.

For N equal to 21, the 5,030 closes give 5,009 overlapping windows. The mean 21-day return is +0.824% and the standard deviation is 4.716%. The 1st percentile is −14.81% and the 99th percentile is +11.24%. The median, +1.45%, sits well above the mean, which is the first sign that the distribution leans to the left. The worst window lost 33.14%, starting on 2020-02-21, and the best gained 25.19%, starting on 2020-03-23, a month later.

## Q2. The shape against a normal

The 21-day returns have a skewness of −1.19 and an excess kurtosis of +5.87. For daily returns the skewness is close to zero at −0.01, but the excess kurtosis is +14.56, which is well above 3, as the assignment's check says it should be.

What this means is that the distribution is not normal in two specific ways. It has fat tails: compared with a normal of the same mean and standard deviation, it has a taller, narrower peak, thinner shoulders and far more extreme observations. And the extremes are lopsided toward losses. A normal distribution would put about 6.8 windows more than 3 standard deviations below the mean and the same number above. The data has 57 below and 14 above. Beyond 4 standard deviations a normal expects 0.2 windows on each side, and the data has 27 below and 4 above. One-month falls beyond 4 standard deviations are more than a hundred times as frequent as a normal says, and large falls are much more common than large rises. The 21-day kurtosis is lower than the daily one because summing days averages out some of the shocks, but it does not get close to zero, because big down days cluster in crises and pile up inside the same month.

The plot q2_return_distribution.png shows the histogram with the fitted normal drawn over it, once on an ordinary scale and once on a log density scale. The log panel makes the left tail obvious: the normal curve has all but vanished by about −17%, while the data keeps going out to −33%.

## Q3. Pricing from history

The function price_from_history takes the price history, the spot, the strike, the number of days and the kind of option. It applies every historical N-day return to today's spot to build 5,009 possible final prices, computes the payoff of the option under each one, and returns the plain average. There is no distribution, volatility or model anywhere in it.

For a 21-day call struck 3% above the spot of $760.88, the strike is $783.71. The historical call price is $5.36 and the matching put at the same strike is $21.92.

The assignment's check says a call and a put at the same strike should differ by roughly spot minus strike, which here is −$22.83. The call minus the put is −$16.55. The $6.27 gap is not an error: it is exactly the historical drift. Inside this method the call minus the put equals the average final price minus the strike, and the average final price is $767.15, not $760.88, because SPY rose 0.82% in the average 21-day window over the last 20 years.

I have not discounted for the risk-free rate. The average payoff is a value received in 21 trading days, so it should be multiplied by e to the power of minus rT before being called a price today. At the 13-week T-bill rate of 4.122% the discount factor for 21/252 of a year is 0.99657, which would take the call to $5.34 and the put to $21.84. So leaving out discounting overstates both prices by about 0.34%, two cents on the call and seven cents on the put. That is small at one month but grows in proportion to both the rate and the maturity. The method has a second, related omission that matters more: it uses the drift SPY actually had, about 10% a year, rather than the risk-free rate less the dividend yield. A properly risk-neutral one-month forward would be about $762.80, not $767.15. Using the higher real-world drift makes calls too expensive and puts too cheap, by up to the $4.35 difference in forwards.

## Q4. The distribution of final prices

The 5,009 final prices run from $508.69 to $952.51. The call struck at $783.71 would have finished in the money in 31.02% of the historical windows, which is 1,554 of 5,009, and the matching put in the other 68.98%, which is 3,455 windows. When the call pays it pays $17.28 on average. When the put pays it pays $31.77 on average, because the put's paying region includes the long left tail.

The plot q4_final_prices.png shows the histogram of final prices twice, once for the call and once for the put, with the strike marked by a solid line, the spot by a dashed line, and the windows that pay off shaded.

## Q5. Black-Scholes on the same two contracts

I used the black_scholes function from Homework 6, imported directly from that folder, with the realised volatility of the last 60 daily log returns as the volatility input. Those 60 returns run from 2026-08-21 to 2026-11-13 and give an annualised volatility of 14.92%. The time to expiry is 21/252 of a year. Because the historical prices are not discounted, the like-for-like Black-Scholes price uses a rate of zero, so neither side is discounted.

For the call struck at $783.71, the historical price is $5.36 and Black-Scholes gives $4.86, so the historical price is 10.3% higher. For the put at the same strike, the historical price is $21.92 and Black-Scholes gives $27.69, so the historical price is 20.8% lower. As a sensitivity check, Black-Scholes at the 4.122% T-bill rate gives $5.54 for the call and $25.68 for the put. At the 20-year sample's own volatility of 16.81% it gives $6.23 and $29.05.

Most of this gap is the drift again rather than volatility. Black-Scholes at a zero rate centres the final price on today's spot, while the historical method centres it $6.27 higher. That alone pushes the historical call up and the historical put down by several dollars. If I remove the drift by converting the historical prices into an implied volatility around their own forward, the 3%-out-of-the-money call comes out at 13.26%, below the 14.92% input. So on volatility and shape alone, history would price this call slightly cheaper than Black-Scholes, because SPY's upside moves are smaller than a normal distribution would predict. The call only ends up more expensive because of the drift.

## Q6. Across strikes from 15% below spot to 15% above

I priced calls and puts at 31 strikes, from 0.85 times spot ($646.75) to 1.15 times spot ($875.01) in steps of 1%, with both methods. The plot q6_strike_comparison.png has four panels: the put prices and the call prices from each method against strike, the difference historical minus Black-Scholes against strike for calls and puts, and the volatility implied by each historical price.

Measured in dollars, the two methods disagree most for the call struck at $707.62, 7% below spot, where the historical price is $7.44 above Black-Scholes. That number has two parts. Inside each method put-call parity holds against its own forward, so every call's dollar difference is the same-strike put's difference plus the $6.27 drift gap. The $7.44 is $6.27 of drift plus $1.17 of extra value in the out-of-the-money put at that strike. If I look only at out-of-the-money options, the largest dollar gap is $3.50 at the money, which is again mostly drift.

The real disagreement shows up in relative terms, in the deep out-of-the-money puts. At 15% below spot, the historical put is worth $0.443 and the Black-Scholes put is worth $0.0006, so history prices it 772 times higher. At 10% below spot the ratio is 13. Across all puts struck 10% or more below spot, the historical price is between 13 and 772 times the Black-Scholes price. Black-Scholes with 14.9% volatility treats a 15% fall in a month as roughly a 3.8 standard deviation event, which a normal distribution says should almost never happen. History says it happened in about 1% of all windows: in late 2008, in early 2009, in August 2011 and in March 2020.

The implied volatility panel shows the same thing as a curve. Read through Black-Scholes, the historical prices imply 28.9% volatility at 15% below spot, 23.6% at 10% below, 15.0% at the money, a minimum of 13.2% at 4% above spot, and 17.8% at 15% above. Black-Scholes is a flat line at 14.9%. The two agree at the money and for calls a little above spot. They disagree sharply on the left, and modestly on the far right, where the rebounds of late 2008, 2009, 2020 and 2025 make large rises somewhat more common than a normal allows.

This is the same reason the Homework 6 implied volatility curve had its shape. In the live SPY chain the implied volatility was 29.2% at 10% below spot, 16.0% at the money and 12.0% at 5% above, a skew that falls from left to right and turns up slightly at the far right. The curve built purely from 20 years of history has the same shape: 23.6% at 10% below, 15.0% at the money and about 13.3% at 5% above. The market is pricing what this distribution shows, a fat and lopsided left tail that a lognormal model with one volatility cannot produce. The market's left side is steeper still, which is consistent with investors paying a premium on top of the historical frequency for crash protection.

## Q7. Written

The two methods agree where the middle of the distribution decides the price. At the money and for calls up to about 10% above spot, the volatility implied by the historical prices is between 13% and 16%, within about two volatility points of the 14.9% Black-Scholes input. They disagree in two places. The first is the centre of the distribution: the historical method carries SPY's real-world drift of about 0.8% a month, which shifts every call up and every put down by roughly $6. That is a choice of measure, not a disagreement about risk, and it is the main reason the 3% contracts differ by 10% and 21%. The second, and the important one, is the left tail. Black-Scholes, fed with a calm 60-day volatility of 14.9%, prices a put 15% out of the money at less than a tenth of a cent. History prices it at 44 cents, 772 times as much, because one-month falls of that size have actually happened, more than once.

For a contract 20% out of the money I would not trust either number on its own, but I would trust the historical method more on the put side, because Black-Scholes is clearly wrong there. A 20% fall in a month is about a 5.2 standard deviation event at 14.9% volatility, so Black-Scholes prices the 20%-out-of-the-money put at $0.000001. SPY has fallen more than 20% in 21 trading days in 22 of the 5,009 windows, so the historical price of $0.185 is at least the right order of magnitude, and the live skew from Homework 6 shows the market agrees with history rather than with the flat-volatility model. On the call side both methods say nearly zero, $0.0136 from history and $0.0001 from Black-Scholes. In practice I would use the historical number as evidence that the Black-Scholes number is far too low, and price the contract from the market's own skew or from a model with fat tails, rather than treat either figure as precise.

One specific weakness of the historical method that Black-Scholes does not have is that it cannot react to current conditions. It applies the same 20-year distribution whether the market is calm or in a panic, so it would charge the same price for a one-month put in a quiet November as in the week after a crash. Black-Scholes, through its volatility input, at least conditions on how the market has moved recently. The historical method also assigns exactly zero probability to anything worse than the worst month in the sample, a 33% fall, so it can never price a crash larger than one that has already happened.

The overlapping windows make the sample look much larger than it is. There are 5,009 windows, but consecutive windows share 20 of their 21 days, so the sample holds only about 238 non-overlapping months. In the tail that matters for a deep out-of-the-money put, it is far worse. The 49 windows with a fall of more than 15% come from only 4 separate episodes: autumn 2008, early 2009, August 2011 and March 2020. The 22 windows with a fall of more than 20% come from just 2 episodes, the 2008 crash and the 2020 crash. So the effective sample for the 20%-out-of-the-money put is two events, not 22 observations and certainly not 5,009. The price moves a lot with the sample: if I recompute the historical price on each of the 21 disjoint subsamples that use only every 21st window, the 15%-out-of-the-money put ranges from $0.09 to $0.94 and the 20% put ranges from $0.00 to $0.62. For the 3%-out-of-the-money call the same exercise gives a narrow range of $4.58 to $6.21. The historical method is reliable in the body of the distribution and statistically fragile in exactly the tail where it disagrees most with Black-Scholes.
